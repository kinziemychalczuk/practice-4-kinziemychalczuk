Piet Mondrian

Dutch pioneer of abstract art, who developed from early landscape pictures to geometric abstract works of a most rigorous kind.

Born in Amersfoort, Utrecht. Studied painting at the Amsterdam Academy 1829-4 and again, part time, 1896-7.

Friendship with the painter Simon Mans and painted landscapes in the Hague School tradition.

1872 - Born in Amersfoort, the Netherlands

1912 - Moves to Paris and sees Cubism

1917 - Co-funds De Stijl with Theo van Doesburg

1930 - Paints Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow

1944 - Dies in New York

"I don't want pictures. I just want to find things out."

For Piet Mondrian, abstract painting was the means of achieving an equilibrium between "concrete" (the tangible and specific aspects of reality percieved by the senses) and the "universal" (the underlying, essential truths that he believed were constant and unchanging).

"The first aim in a painting should be universal expression,"

he told the MoMA curator James Johnson Sweeney toward the end of his life. "The second aim should be concrete, universal expression."

He believed deeply in progress, and was convinced that his art was moving toward achieving a synthesis of the two terms in a way that would precipitate a utopian future in which the distinction between art and life would be dissolved.

Boogie-woogie

- a type of blues undergoing a resurgence in the 1940s - was especially important to Mondrian's artistic thinking in New York. Toward the end of his life, he worked almost exclusively on two paintings - Broadway Boogie-Woogie and Victory Boogie-Woogie.

As the titles suggest, music was critical to their conception. Each painting is constructed using lines of shifting color, which interrupt each other, blink, and dance. There is a dynamism in these canvases that escapes any static element remaining in Mondrian's art; they are a testament to the vibrant sonic life of New York. "The great struggle for the artist is the annihilation of static equilibrium in their paintings...Abstract art is a concrete expression of such a vitality."

Lines, colors and the rule

Lines are only horizontal and vertical. Mondrian painted them by hand, and up close the edges are not perfectly straight. They stop short of the canvas edge in places, which keeps the grid from feeling like a cage.

Red, blue and yellow, plus black, white and grey. Never mixed, never shaded. Each color sits flat in its own rectangle, so the picture is read as relationships between areas rather than as objects.

The rule: no symmetry. If the red square were centered the picture would go still. Pushing it to one corner and answering it with two small blocks at the bottom is what makes the whole thing balance.
